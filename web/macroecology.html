Macroecology — medicinal plant diversity at Mekong scale

A global analysis of medicinal plant diversity across 369 botanical countries1 identified human occupancy time as its second-most important predictor. This page reproduces the residual analysis on the published data for the seven botanical countries that contain the Mekong basin, and extends it to sub-national (province) scale.

Reading the Mekong through the global framework

The Mekong’s documented medicinal flora sits on one of the world’s oldest continuously occupied cultural landscapes. All seven botanical countries that contain the basin carry more medicinal species than their vascular floras alone would predict, and six of the seven do so significantly (residuals +994 to +2,195; p ≤ 0.072). This is what Shrestha et al.’s global model would lead one to expect: human occupancy time was the second-most important predictor of medicinal plant diversity in their 369-country analysis, and Indomalaya — the realm in which the basin sits — has the steepest vascular-to-medicinal slope of the six realms (~310 medicinal species per 1,000 vascular species, versus ~106 in Australasia). The basin is the demographic heart of five major language families — Austroasiatic, Tai-Kadai, Tibeto-Burman, Hmong-Mien and Austronesian — and the meeting place of several canonical medical systems: Traditional Chinese Medicine in Yunnan and Guangxi, Thai traditional medicine, Vietnamese thuốc nam, Khmer traditional medicine, and the folk pharmacopoeias of upland Lao, Hmong, Khmu, Karen, Chin and Kachin communities. Long occupancy multiplied by many distinct medical traditions is the mechanism the global analysis points to, and the basin’s residuals are its regional signature. Language diversity per se appears to act only indirectly through climate in the global model, so the atlas maps languages as context, not as a predictor. The one exception worth attention is Cambodia: its residual (+994) is the smallest in the basin and not significant at p < 0.05, and Cambodia is also the country with the fewest occurrence records and the thinnest literature coverage in the atlas. That combination — a modest global residual, low documentation density, and a rich Khmer therapeutic tradition — marks Cambodia, and its upland provinces in particular, as the basin’s leading under-documentation hypothesis, which the internal coldspot analysis below tests at sub-national scale.

Methods and reproducibility

  • Data: Shrestha et al. 2025 repository (nawalshrestha/med_plants, data.csv, reused under the paper’s DOI and commit hash recorded in the source register); WCVP (sftp.kew.org, retrieved 2026-11-25); WGSRPD level-3 boundaries (tdwg/wgsrpd); Glottolog (Zenodo record 24104033); Natural Earth 10m admin-1; GBIF occurrence API (vascular species counts per province, taxonKey=6).
  • Model: ordinary least squares, med_rich ~ vas_rich, over all botanical countries except Antarctica and Oceania (the authors’ exclusion). Significance: |residual| > 1.96 SD (the authors’ threshold in residual_histogram.R); the table reports the two-tailed normal-approximation p.
  • Extension: the same model refit at admin-1 scale inside the basin using GBIF vascular species counts per province and the atlas’s documented medicinal taxa per province. Differences from the global model are documented in the manuscript.
  • Scripts: scripts/macroecology.py, scripts/macroecology_layers.py, scripts/wcvp_match.py, scripts/harvest_admin1_vascular.py, scripts/internal_coldspots.py. Inputs cached under data/raw/shrestha2025/.
  • MPNS v12: mpns.science.kew.org was unavailable (Cloudflare 5xx) on 2026-11-25; MPNS membership badges show as pending and the country-level medicinal values come from the published dataset, which is MPNS-derived.
